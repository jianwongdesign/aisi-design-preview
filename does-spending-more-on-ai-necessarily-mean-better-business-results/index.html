Does spending more on AI necessarily mean better business results?

Singapore firms may be losing 23% of their AI budgets to complexity. Here are four practical ways SMEs can avoid disconnected tools, stalled pilots and unnecessary AI costs.

In this article

According to a recent Singapore Business Review article, Singapore’s mid-market firms lose an estimated 23% of their AI budgets to complexity before seeing returns. (Source: https://sbr.com.sg/news/singapore-mid-market-firms-lose-23-ai-budgets-complexity)

While 92% plan to increase their AI investment, only 20% have integrated AI into their core operations. Another 28% are still stuck at the pilot stage.

Although the research focused on mid-market companies, the message is equally important for SMEs:

The biggest cost of AI may not be the technology. It may be the complexity surrounding it.

Where Does the AI Budget Go?

AI projects often begin with an attractive demonstration.

A chatbot answers questions. An AI tool extracts information from documents. An agent prepares a report in seconds.

But moving from a demonstration to daily operations can be difficult.

Businesses may encounter:

  • AI tools that do not connect with existing systems
  • Too many separate tools and subscriptions
  • Employees manually copying information between platforms
  • AI outputs that require repeated checking and correction
  • Unexpected configuration and development work
  • Pilots that never progress into actual deployment

These hidden costs can quickly reduce the expected return from AI.

Why AI Projects Become Too Complicated

1. Starting With the Tool, Not the Problem

A business may decide that it needs an AI chatbot or AI agent without first identifying the problem it wants to solve.

A better starting point is a clear operational challenge:

  • Reports take too long to prepare.
  • Customer enquiries are answered slowly.
  • Employees spend hours searching for information.
  • Sales teams manually update customer records.
  • Documents and certificates are tracked using spreadsheets.

Once the problem is clear, the business can decide whether AI is the right solution—and what type of AI is required.

2. Adding AI Without Improving the Workflow

AI cannot fully solve an inefficient process if the remaining steps are still manual.

For example, an AI tool may extract information from an invoice. But an employee may still need to copy the information into a spreadsheet, email it for approval and enter it into an accounting system.

The better approach is to improve the complete workflow:

  1. AI extracts the information.
  2. The system checks for missing or unusual details.
  3. A person reviews only the exceptions.
  4. Approved information is entered into the correct system.
  5. Follow-up tasks or notifications are created automatically.

The goal is not simply to use AI. It is to make the entire process work better.

3. Using Too Many Separate AI Tools

Different departments may start buying different AI applications.

One tool is used for writing, another for documents, another for customer service and another for automation.

Over time, this creates:

  • More subscriptions
  • More employee training
  • Repeated data uploads
  • Different security controls
  • Unclear ownership
  • Higher IT support requirements

Before purchasing another tool, businesses should ask:

Does this solve an important problem, and can it work with the systems we already use?

4. Expecting Results Too Quickly

Around 73% of the Singapore executives surveyed expected AI to show returns within eight months. However, 48% said deployment alone can take between six and 12 months.

Some focused AI projects can produce value quickly. Others require data preparation, system integration, testing and employee training.

Businesses should therefore separate the journey into stages:

  • Proof of concept
  • Operational pilot
  • Production deployment
  • Wider adoption
  • Measurable return on investment

A successful demonstration is only the first step.

How SMEs Can Avoid the AI Complexity Trap

SMEs do not need a large AI programme to begin seeing results.

They can start with four practical steps.

Start With One Valuable Problem

Choose a repetitive process that creates delays, errors or unnecessary work.

Focus on a problem that can be clearly measured.

Simplify Before Automating

Remove unnecessary steps and approvals before introducing AI.

Automating a complicated process may only create a faster complicated process.

Connect AI to Existing Workflows

Where possible, use AI within the platforms employees already use, such as Microsoft Teams, SharePoint, CRM systems or operational applications.

Employees should not have to manually connect every stage of the process.

Measure the Complete Result

Do not measure only how quickly the AI generates an answer.

Also measure:

  • Total time saved
  • Errors and corrections
  • Manual work removed
  • Employee adoption
  • Operating costs
  • Customer or business outcomes

A report generated in one minute creates little value if an employee spends another 30 minutes correcting it.

Spend Better Before Spending More

The lesson from the 23% figure is not that businesses should stop investing in AI.

It is that they should invest more carefully.

The best AI solution is not always the most advanced or expensive. It is the one that solves a real problem, fits into existing operations and produces measurable value.

Before making the next AI investment, ask:

  • What business problem are we solving?
  • What manual work will be removed?
  • How will the AI connect with our current systems?
  • Who will review and manage it?
  • How will we measure whether it works?

Businesses that manage these questions well will be better positioned to turn AI investment into real operational results.
