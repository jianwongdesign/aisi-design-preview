Can Your Employees Put Customer Data into ChatGPT? A Practical PDPA Guide for Singapore SMEs

Employees are using ChatGPT and other AI tools to work faster—but what happens when customer information is entered? Learn how Singapore SMEs can use generative AI responsibly while protecting personal and confidential data.

An employee receives a long customer email and asks ChatGPT to summarise it.

A sales executive uploads a spreadsheet so an AI tool can identify the best prospects.

An HR manager copies a job applicant’s résumé into an AI assistant to prepare interview questions.

These actions may save time. But they may also expose personal, confidential or commercially sensitive information to an external AI system.

As generative AI tools become part of everyday work, Singapore SMEs need to answer an increasingly important question:

What information should employees be allowed to enter into ChatGPT and other AI tools?

The answer is not that businesses must stop using AI. It is that they need clear rules, approved tools and appropriate safeguards.

The Short Answer: It Depends on the Information and the AI Tool

Customer data should not automatically be entered into a public or unapproved AI tool.

Whether an AI use is appropriate depends on several factors:

  • What information is being entered?
  • Does it identify an individual?
  • Is the information confidential?
  • Why does the AI need the information?
  • Has unnecessary information been removed?
  • How does the AI provider store, process and retain submitted data?
  • Has the tool been reviewed and approved by the company?
  • Will a person check the AI-generated result?

This means the same AI tool may be suitable for one task but unsuitable for another.

For example, asking AI to improve a generic marketing paragraph is very different from uploading a customer contract containing names, contact details, pricing and payment information.

Why This Matters Under Singapore’s PDPA

Singapore’s Personal Data Protection Act, or PDPA, governs how organisations collect, use, disclose and protect personal data.

In July 2026, the Personal Data Protection Commission issued its Advisory Guidelines on the Use of Personal Data in Generative AI. The guidelines clarify how existing data protection responsibilities apply during the development, deployment and use of generative AI systems.

Some of the important areas covered include:

  • Accountability for how personal data is handled
  • Consent and notification requirements
  • Appropriate purposes for using personal data
  • Protection of personal data
  • Retention of personal data
  • Data minimisation and anonymisation
  • Responsibilities across AI providers, system providers and organisations deploying AI
  • Handling requests relating to an individual’s personal data

For SMEs, the practical message is straightforward:

Using an AI tool does not remove the company’s responsibility to protect personal data.

The organisation should understand what information is entering the AI system, why it is being used and what happens to it afterwards.

What Is Customer Data?

Customer data is a broad term. It can include information such as:

  • Names
  • Personal email addresses
  • Mobile numbers
  • Home addresses
  • Identification numbers
  • Photographs and video recordings
  • Transaction histories
  • Account information
  • Payment records
  • Customer complaints
  • Medical or insurance information
  • Recorded calls and meeting transcripts
  • Information that identifies a person when combined with other details

Some business contact information may be treated differently under the PDPA. However, a customer document often contains much more than a name, job title and business telephone number.

A quotation, contract or email chain may also reveal purchasing behaviour, financial information, personal circumstances or confidential commercial terms.

Employees should therefore avoid assuming that an entire document is safe simply because it relates to business.

Everyday Situations That Can Create Risk

Employees do not normally intend to expose information. Most incidents happen because someone is trying to complete a task more quickly.

Here are some common examples.

Summarising a Customer Email

A customer service employee copies an entire email conversation into an AI tool and asks it to prepare a reply.

The conversation may contain the customer’s name, contact details, order history, payment information or complaint.

A safer approach may be to remove identifying details first or use a company-approved AI environment that has been assessed for the task.

Analysing a Sales Spreadsheet

A sales manager uploads a customer list and asks AI to rank which customers are most likely to purchase again.

The spreadsheet may contain personal data, commercial information and sensitive sales history.

The company should first determine whether the tool is approved, whether all the information is necessary and whether the data can be anonymised or aggregated.

Reviewing Résumés

An HR employee enters a candidate’s résumé into an AI system to generate interview questions.

Résumés can contain names, addresses, telephone numbers, employment histories, qualifications and other personal information.

The organisation should consider whether it has an appropriate basis for using the information in this way and whether the candidate has been adequately informed.

AI-generated assessments should also be reviewed carefully to avoid inaccurate or unfair conclusions.

Preparing Meeting Notes

An employee uploads a recording or transcript of a meeting so AI can produce a summary.

The discussion may include employee information, customer details, pricing, business strategies or confidential project information.

Meeting participants may not expect the conversation to be processed by an external AI service.

Drafting General Marketing Content

An employee asks AI to draft a social media post about a new service without entering customer information or confidential company material.

This is generally a lower-risk use case, although the output should still be checked for accuracy, copyright concerns and inappropriate claims.

Seven Questions Employees Should Ask Before Using AI

Before entering information into any generative AI tool, employees should ask:

1. Does the Information Identify Anyone?

Check for names, contact details, photographs, identification numbers and other information that may identify an individual.

Do not look only at the main document. Attachments, comments and background information may also contain personal data.

2. Is the Information Confidential?

Not all sensitive information is personal data.

Pricing, source code, contracts, product plans, financial projections, passwords and internal reports may be commercially confidential even if they do not identify an individual.

3. Does the AI Really Need This Information?

An AI tool may not need the customer’s real name, telephone number or account number to summarise an enquiry.

Where possible, remove unnecessary information before submitting the content.

4. Can the Information Be Anonymised?

Replace names and identifying details with neutral labels such as:

  • Customer A
  • Supplier B
  • Employee 1
  • Project X

However, simply removing a name may not be enough if the remaining details can still identify the person.

5. Is This an Approved AI Tool?

Employees should know which AI tools the company has approved and what each tool may be used for.

A free personal account and a company-managed AI service may have different settings, controls and contractual terms.

Businesses should not assume that every enterprise-labelled AI product is automatically suitable. The provider’s privacy, security, retention and data-use terms should still be reviewed.

6. What Happens to the Information After It Is Submitted?

Depending on the service, account type and settings, submitted information may be stored, logged, reviewed or retained for a period of time.

The company should understand:

  • Where information is processed
  • Who can access it
  • How long it is retained
  • Whether it may be used to improve or train models
  • Whether data use can be disabled
  • What security controls are available
  • What happens when an account is closed

7. Would You Be Comfortable Explaining This Use?

If an employee would be uncomfortable explaining the AI use to a customer, manager or Data Protection Officer, the information should not be submitted until approval is obtained.

When in doubt, stop and check.

A Simple Traffic-Light Guide for Employees

SMEs can make their AI rules easier to follow by using a traffic-light system.

Green: Generally Lower Risk

Examples include:

  • Publicly available company information
  • Generic marketing ideas
  • Non-confidential templates
  • Fictional examples
  • Content that contains no personal or sensitive information
  • Rewriting or proofreading general text

Employees should still check AI-generated outputs before using them.

Amber: Approval or Additional Safeguards Needed

Examples include:

  • Internal reports
  • Meeting notes
  • Supplier information
  • Draft contracts
  • Operational data
  • Anonymised customer information
  • Internal policies and procedures

These may be used only with approved tools and appropriate safeguards.

Red: Do Not Enter into an Unapproved AI Tool

Examples include:

  • Identification numbers
  • Payroll records
  • Bank and payment information
  • Passwords and access credentials
  • Medical information
  • Customer databases
  • Unredacted résumés
  • Confidential contracts
  • Source code
  • Sensitive photographs or recordings
  • Legally privileged information
  • Information classified as restricted by the company

Red-category information should require formal approval and a properly assessed AI environment.

What Singapore SMEs Should Put in Place

AI governance does not need to begin with a lengthy policy or an expensive compliance programme.

A practical starting point includes the following steps.

1. Create a List of Approved AI Tools

Specify which tools employees may use and which accounts or subscription plans are required.

The list should also explain the approved purpose of each tool.

For example, a tool approved for public marketing content may not be approved for analysing customer records.

2. Introduce a One-Page Employee AI Policy

The policy should explain:

  • What employees may and may not enter
  • Which tools are approved
  • When approval is required
  • Who is responsible for checking AI outputs
  • How employees should report a mistake
  • Who to contact when they are unsure

Clear instructions are more useful than a policy employees cannot understand.

3. Minimise the Data

Only provide the information needed to complete the task.

Remove names, contact details and unrelated fields wherever possible.

Uploading an entire customer file when only one paragraph is required creates unnecessary exposure.

4. Review AI Providers Properly

Before approving an AI service, SMEs should review its:

  • Privacy policy
  • Security controls
  • Data retention practices
  • Training-data policy
  • Access controls
  • Administrative settings
  • Incident response arrangements
  • Contractual terms

The appropriate review will depend on the sensitivity of the information and the importance of the business process.

5. Keep Humans in the Loop

AI-generated answers can be inaccurate, incomplete or inappropriate.

A responsible employee should review important outputs before they are sent to customers, entered into business systems or used to make decisions.

Human review is especially important for financial, legal, employment, safety and compliance-related tasks.

6. Train Employees Using Real Examples

Employees need more than a warning not to share sensitive information.

Training should show them how to:

  • Recognise personal and confidential data
  • Remove unnecessary identifiers
  • Use approved tools
  • Review AI-generated outputs
  • Escalate uncertain situations
  • Report accidental disclosures quickly

Use examples from the company’s actual workflows so the rules are easy to apply.

7. Prepare for Mistakes

If an employee accidentally enters sensitive information into an unapproved AI system, they should report it immediately.

The company can then assess:

  • What information was involved
  • Which AI service received it
  • Whether the information can be deleted
  • Whether account settings can be changed
  • Whether access credentials need to be reset
  • Whether the incident must be escalated to the Data Protection Officer
  • Whether any further notification or response is required

Employees should not hide mistakes because they are afraid of being blamed. Fast reporting gives the company more time to reduce the impact.

Do Not Ban AI Without Providing an Alternative

Some businesses respond to AI risk by banning all generative AI tools.

This may appear simple, but it can drive AI use underground. Employees may continue using personal accounts because the tools help them work faster.

A more practical approach is to provide:

  • Approved AI tools
  • Clear usage boundaries
  • Appropriate security settings
  • Employee training
  • A process for requesting new use cases
  • Additional safeguards for sensitive information

The goal is not to stop employees from using AI.

It is to help them use AI productively, safely and responsibly.

Responsible AI Starts With Clear Rules

Generative AI can help SMEs draft documents, summarise information, automate administrative work and respond to customers more efficiently.

But convenience should not come at the expense of customer trust.

Before allowing employees to use AI with business information, SMEs should understand their data, assess their tools and establish clear internal rules.

Start with three questions:

  • What information are employees entering?
  • Which AI systems are they using?
  • What safeguards are currently in place?

If the business cannot answer these questions, it may already have an unmanaged AI risk.
