Your Staff Already Adopted AI. Your Company Didn’t.

Most Singapore SMEs already have AI in use, on free tiers and personal accounts, with nobody owning it. Here’s what that costs you and what to do first.

Two conversations in the past few months, with two very different companies. One is a 30-person firm in financial services. The other moves consumer goods and has around 130 staff.

We asked both what AI tools their people were using. Neither could say.

Not because they were hiding anything. They didn’t know. Both could tell us exactly what their teams were getting from it: hours back on drafting and reporting, a few manual processes that had quietly stopped being manual. The results were visible. The system producing them wasn’t, because there wasn’t one.

If you run an SME in Singapore, there’s a fair chance you’d struggle with the same question.

The adoption already happened. You just weren’t in the room.

The usual story is that SMEs are behind on AI and need to start. IMDA’s figures show adoption climbing fast, from 4.2% to 14.5% among SMEs in a single year. But those numbers count what companies report at an organisational level. They don’t count what your staff started doing on their own eighteen months ago.

Someone in marketing got tired of writing the same product descriptions. Someone in ops worked out that a weekly report could be reformatted in thirty seconds instead of forty minutes. No purchase request. No word to IT. It cost nothing, so it never entered a budget, and because it never entered a budget it was never a decision anyone made.

That isn’t people going around the rules. In most SMEs there were no rules to go around.

So what’s actually the problem?

In both companies, the picture was the same: a mix of free tiers and individual subscriptions staff signed up for themselves. Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, whichever one a colleague recommended. Good tools, all of them. The problem isn’t the software. It’s the arrangement.

The account isn’t yours.

When someone subscribes personally, the account belongs to them, and so does everything in it. The conversation history. The saved instructions. The prompt they finally got right after two months so the report comes out in your house format.

That’s institutional knowledge sitting in an account your company can’t access and can’t recover. When that person resigns, it walks out with them, and there’s nothing to hand over, because the workflow was never written down. It only ever existed as a habit. You wouldn’t accept this for a customer list.

The data went somewhere you have no agreement with.

Free consumer tiers are built for individuals, not organisations. No admin view of who’s using what. No audit trail. No way to switch off access when someone leaves, because the account was never yours to switch off.

Meanwhile the material going in is real. Client names in a drafted email. Figures from a sales report. Supplier terms pasted in for a quick summary. Sometimes personal data, which under the PDPA stays your organisation’s responsibility no matter which tool an employee happened to pick. The obligation is yours. The visibility isn’t. (We went through this in more detail in our guide to what employees can and can’t put into ChatGPT.)

And you’re already paying for it.

Ask most SME owners what the company spends on AI a year and the answer is near zero, or one official subscription. The real number is usually higher, because individual subscriptions get bought individually, at full price, sometimes expensed and sometimes just absorbed. It’s rarely a large sum, which is why nobody catches it. But a cost nobody can see is a cost nobody is accountable for, and that’s true of everything else on this list too.

Where is your company, really?

Four rough stages. Nobody using it. A couple of individuals using it. Lots of individuals using it separately. Or a company system, where accounts are shared and controlled, use is agreed, and workflows belong to the business rather than to whoever built them.

Both companies we spoke to were at stage three and had been for over a year without noticing.

Stage three is the trap, because it works. People are faster. Things get done. From the owner’s chair it looks like adoption is going fine. But nothing compounds. Two departments solve the same problem twice and neither one learns from the other. The gains are capped at what one motivated person can manage alone, and they reset when that person leaves.

It feels like momentum. It behaves like a plateau.

Why nobody fixes it

Because it isn’t anyone’s job. It’s not IT’s, since nothing was bought. It’s not the staff member’s, since designing a company-wide system was never in their role, and honestly they did the right thing by finding something that worked. And it rarely reaches the MD as a decision, because from the top it doesn’t look like a problem. It looks like people being productive.

So it sits there. The exposure builds up quietly and the capability doesn’t.

What to do on Monday

Before any AI project, any vendor meeting, any budget: find out what’s already in use.

Ask every department four questions. What AI tools are you using? What for? Whose account is it on? Who pays for it?

Make it clear that nobody’s in trouble. If people think they’re being audited, you’ll get a clean sheet and learn nothing. Frame it as what it is, which is that you want to build on whatever’s already working.

Most owners who do this get surprised twice. First by how much is going on. Then by how good some of it is. That second surprise is the real finding. Your staff have already worked out which parts of the business benefit most from automation. They ran the pilot for you, for free, without being asked.

You’re not starting from zero. You’re taking what they proved and making it something the company owns.
